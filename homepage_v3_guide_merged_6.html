The Quick Guide

Three short reads. Everything you need before your first bottle — or your fiftieth.

1
How to Taste

Prep your palate first. Don't taste whiskey right after coffee, mint gum, or a spicy meal — your palate is already occupied. A neutral mouth tastes more.

Start with a micro-sip. Not a full sip — just enough to coat your tongue. Your palate needs a moment to adjust to the alcohol before it can taste anything else. The first sip is always going to taste like alcohol. That's normal, not a verdict on the whiskey.

Add a few drops of water. This isn't cheating — professional tasters do it deliberately. A little water lowers the proof enough to tame the ethanol burn and physically opens up aromas that were locked behind the alcohol. Start with 2-3 drops for a 1.5oz pour.

Then try it neat, actually neat. Once your palate's warmed up, taste it with no ice and no water. That's the whiskey as the distiller intended — but it's the third step, not the first.

2
Decode the Label

Every label has a few numbers you'll see every time, plus a handful of legally regulated phrases that show up more selectively. Knowing both tells you something real about what's in the bottle.

ON EVERY LABEL

ProofStrength, not quality. Divide by 2 for the actual alcohol percentage (100 proof = 50% ABV). Higher proof isn't automatically better — just more concentrated.
AgeIf a number's on the label, that's the youngest whiskey inside, not the oldest. No age statement doesn't mean no age — it often means blended across years on purpose.

LEGAL TERMS WORTH KNOWING

Bottled-in-BondA legal guarantee: aged at least 4 years, bottled at exactly 100 proof, made at one distillery in one season. One of the strongest quality signals on any label.
Single BarrelComes from one specific barrel, not blended with others. More variation bottle-to-bottle, often more character.
Straight BourbonAged at least 2 years with no coloring or flavoring added. "Straight" is a legal claim, not a style descriptor.
Single Malt100% malted barley from one distillery — "single" means one distillery, not one barrel. It can still be a blend of many barrels and years from that same place.
3
Know Your Styles

Some of this comes down to where a whiskey is made. Some comes down to what it's made from. American Whiskey is the umbrella term — Bourbon and Rye are its two most common styles, not separate categories from it. Seven starting points:

🗺️PHOTO / ICON
American Whiskey The umbrella category — Bourbon and Rye are its two biggest styles, both made under US federal rules.
🌽PHOTO / ICON
Bourbon Corn-forward, sweet, vanilla and caramel from new charred oak. Legally can be made anywhere in the US — Kentucky just makes almost all of it.
🌾PHOTO / ICON
Rye Spicier, drier, peppery — the backbone of a classic Manhattan. Distinct from Canadian "rye," which plays by looser rules.
⛰️PHOTO / ICON
Scotch — Scotland Six distinct regions, from Speyside's fruit to Islay's smoke — same grain, radically different results.
☘️PHOTO / ICON
Irish Whiskey Usually triple-distilled, which is exactly what makes it the smoothest common entry point.
🎌PHOTO / ICON
Japanese Whisky Built on Scotch tradition, refined into its own style. Since 2021, the label now means it was actually distilled in Japan.
🍁PHOTO / ICON
Canadian Whisky Often called "rye" out of habit, even when it isn't mostly rye grain — the most flexible rulebook of any major whisky-producing country.
